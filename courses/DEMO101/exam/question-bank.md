# Question bank: DEMO101

Short-answer questions for practice with `exam-trainer`. Aim for 150 to 250 words per answer, closed book, about ten minutes each. The marking points are folded so you can practise without seeing them.

## 1. Retrieval practice

A student rereads a chapter four times and feels well prepared. Another reads it once and then tests themselves three times. Explain who is likely to remember more a week later, and why the first student's confidence is misleading.

> [!success]- Marking points
> 1. Retrieval strengthens memory more than rereading does. [[how-learning-works#Retrieval practice]]
> 2. The result reverses over time: rereading led after five minutes (83 against 71 percent) and trailed after a week (40 against 61 percent). [[how-learning-works#Retrieval practice]]
> 3. The rereading group was the most confident, so the feeling of knowing is a poor guide. [[how-learning-works#Retrieval practice]]

## 2. Spacing

You have just learned a topic and the exam is in 30 days. Propose a review schedule for the topic and justify it with evidence.

> [!success]- Marking points
> 1. Spaced study beats massed study, found consistently across 317 experiments. [[how-learning-works#Spacing]]
> 2. The best gap depends on the delay: about 20 to 40 percent of it for a test a week away, about 5 to 10 percent for a test a year away. [[how-learning-works#Spacing]]
> 3. A concrete schedule that follows from this: a first review after a day or two, growing gaps after each successful recall, and gaps kept short compared with the time left. [[how-learning-works#Spacing]]

## 3. Interleaving

A classmate argues that practising mixed problem types is inefficient, because they make more mistakes than when they practise one type at a time. Respond using evidence.

> [!success]- Marking points
> 1. More errors during practice is expected: 60 percent correct for mixed practice against 89 for blocked. [[how-learning-works#Interleaving]]
> 2. The later test reverses it: 63 against 20 percent a week later. [[how-learning-works#Interleaving]]
> 3. Learners misjudge this, as in the painters study, where most rated blocked presentation as equally good or better. [[how-learning-works#Interleaving]]
> 4. A limitation is named, such as the small sample of about 18 students. [[how-learning-works#Interleaving]]

## 4. Desirable difficulties

What is a desirable difficulty? Give one example, and explain when a difficulty stops being desirable.

> [!success]- Marking points
> 1. A condition that lowers performance during practice and improves long-term learning. [[how-learning-works#Desirable difficulties]]
> 2. An example: testing, spacing or interleaving. [[how-learning-works#Desirable difficulties]]
> 3. Performance while studying is an unreliable sign of learning. [[how-learning-works#Desirable difficulties]]
> 4. It is not desirable when the learner lacks the background to respond to it. [[how-learning-works#Desirable difficulties]]

## 5. Choosing techniques

A review rated ten study techniques. Name the two it rated highest and two it rated low, and say what this means for how a student should spend an evening.

> [!success]- Marking points
> 1. Rated high: practice testing and distributed practice. [[how-learning-works#Why this matters]]
> 2. Rated low: rereading and highlighting. [[how-learning-works#Why this matters]]
> 3. What feels productive and what works are often different, so the evening should go to self-testing spread over days, with rereading as a first pass only. [[how-learning-works#What this does not say]]
