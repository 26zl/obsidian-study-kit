# How learning works: three techniques that hold up

A short reading for the demo course. It summarises published research. The references are at the end.

## Why this matters

Most students study by rereading and highlighting. A review of ten common study techniques rated both of those as low in usefulness. Only two techniques were rated high: practice testing and distributed practice (Dunlosky et al., 2013). The techniques that feel productive and the techniques that work are often not the same ones.

## Retrieval practice

Pulling information out of memory strengthens it more than taking it in again.

In one experiment, students read prose passages. Some reread the passage several times, and others read it once and then took recall tests, without feedback. Five minutes later the rereading group remembered more: 83 percent against 71 percent. A week later the order had reversed: 61 percent for the students who studied once and tested themselves three times, and 40 percent for those who studied four times. The rereading group had also been the most confident that they would remember (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).

A later study compared retrieval practice with drawing concept maps while looking at the text. One week on, the retrieval group answered 67 percent of the questions correctly and the concept-mapping group 45 percent. The retrieval group also did better when the final test was to draw a concept map. Beforehand, the students had predicted the opposite (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011).

What follows for studying:

- Test yourself before you feel ready.
- Recall from memory, without the text open.
- Do not trust the feeling of knowing that rereading gives.

## Spacing

Study spread over time beats the same amount of study in one block. A synthesis of 317 experiments found this consistently for verbal material (Cepeda et al., 2006).

How long the gap should be depends on how long the material has to be remembered. In an experiment with more than 1,350 people, the best gap between two study sessions was about 20 to 40 percent of the delay when the test came a week later, and about 5 to 10 percent when it came a year later (Cepeda et al., 2008).

What follows for studying:

- Come back to a topic after a day or two, not the same evening.
- Let the gap grow each time the recall succeeds.
- Keep the gap short compared with the time left before the exam.

## Interleaving

Interleaving means mixing different kinds of problems or topics in one session, where blocked practice finishes one kind before starting the next.

Students who practised four kinds of mathematics problems in mixed order did worse during practice than students who practised them in blocks: 60 percent correct against 89 percent. A week later the mixed group scored 63 percent and the blocked group 20 percent (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007). The sample was small, about 18 students.

In another study, people learned to recognise painters' styles. Seeing the painters mixed gave better results on new paintings than seeing each painter's works together: 61 percent against 35 percent on the first test block. Most participants still judged the blocked presentation as equally good or better (Kornell & Bjork, 2008).

A likely reason is that mixed practice makes you decide which method a problem calls for. An exam asks the same of you, and blocked practice lets you skip that step.

## Desirable difficulties

Testing, spacing and interleaving all make practice feel slower and harder. Bjork and Bjork (2011) call them desirable difficulties: conditions that lower performance during practice and improve learning in the long run. Their point is that how well you perform while studying is an unreliable sign of how much you have learned.

A difficulty is only desirable when you have the background to respond to it. A recall test on material you never understood is not practice. It is guessing.

## What this does not say

- It does not say rereading is useless. Rereading is a first pass, and it is a weak way to spend the second and third.
- The effects were measured mostly on recall of texts, facts and problem types, over days and weeks. The sizes vary between studies.
- These are tools. They do not replace understanding the material in the first place.
